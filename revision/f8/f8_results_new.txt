Table 4 reports the metric set on the 19-feature configuration, with the two baselines. Random Forest has the highest point estimate on macro F1, balanced accuracy, G-mean, PR-AUC and QWK; XGBoost on accuracy, MCC, Cohen's kappa and ROC-AUC; the SVM trails on every aggregate metric. The confidence intervals of the three models overlap on every metric, and the ROC-AUC intervals of all three include 0.5. Persistence, which repeats the previous day's label, reaches macro F1 0.304 and accuracy 56.3% but never predicts an Active day correctly (F1 Active 0, G-mean 0).

Pairwise comparisons confirm what the overlapping intervals suggest. McNemar tests on per-day correctness gave Holm-adjusted p-values of 0.40 (RF versus XGBoost), 0.88 (RF versus SVM) and 0.38 (XGBoost versus SVM), and Cochran's Q over the three models gave p = 0.19. Paired bootstrap differences on macro F1 all include zero: RF minus XGBoost 0.030 [-0.080, 0.156], RF minus SVM 0.072 [-0.034, 0.178], XGBoost minus SVM 0.041 [-0.102, 0.165]. The same holds on balanced accuracy. The ordering RF > XGBoost > SVM is therefore an ordering of point estimates on a test set with 8 Active days, not an established difference. Against the persistence baseline, the advantage of Random Forest lies on the minority classes. The paired difference is 0.202 [0.064, 0.317] on macro F1 and 0.219 [0.073, 0.363] on balanced accuracy, both separated from zero. The McNemar test on per-day accuracy does not separate the two (p = 0.13), because persistence obtains much of its accuracy from runs of consecutive Normal days. The full test tables are in Supplementary Table S1.

Table 6 reports the seven classifiers with default hyperparameters, together with the persistence baseline (the previous day's label) and the majority baseline, each evaluated once on the 192 test days. No model exceeded the reported Random Forest on macro F1 at the point estimate. CatBoost came closest (0.498 against 0.506), with a paired bootstrap difference of 0.008 [-0.084, 0.113]. Only TabNet differed from Random Forest beyond the 95% paired interval (difference 0.215 [0.077, 0.335]; McNemar p = 0.0006, Holm-adjusted 0.0025), and it did not exceed persistence. LightGBM with defaults predicted almost only Normal (165 of 192 days), and its difference from persistence included zero. FT-Transformer with defaults exceeded persistence (difference +0.108 [+0.004, +0.210]). Across five model seeds with SMOTE fixed, CatBoost ranged from 0.498 (seed 42, fixed before the run and reported here) to 0.539 and was above the reported Random Forest (0.506) on four of five seeds; the default-parameter comparison therefore does not establish an ordering between the two. Both networks remained below all four tree ensembles on every seed (FT-Transformer at most 0.419, TabNet at most 0.360). With its default batch size of 1,024 on 1,710 oversampled training rows, TabNet saw a single batch per epoch and stopped after five epochs. This result reflects a mismatch between its defaults and the dataset size rather than a property of the architecture.

Hyperparameter search (Appendix A, Table A.1) changed the ordering on the test set, and both orderings are reported. Cross-validation scores were low (0.37 to 0.43 for the selected configurations) and did not rank the models as the test set does: the two networks scored highest on cross-validation and lowest on the test set. Their cross-validation scores are optimistic relative to the tree models, since their number of epochs was selected on the same validation block, which partly explains the reversal. On the test set, tuning raised LightGBM from 0.409 to 0.574 [0.417, 0.680], Random Forest from 0.506 to 0.568 [0.417, 0.676] and CatBoost from 0.498 to 0.537 [0.394, 0.644]. The tuned Random Forest and LightGBM exceed the reported Random Forest by +0.062 [+0.003, +0.140] and +0.068 [+0.000, +0.148]. The lower bounds of these paired intervals lie at or just above zero before any adjustment for the seven comparisons, and no McNemar difference remains after Holm correction. The three tuned tree ensembles are not separated from one another: the differences against the tuned Random Forest are +0.006 [-0.031, +0.045] for LightGBM and -0.031 [-0.081, +0.010] for CatBoost. Across five model seeds their ranges overlap (LightGBM 0.498 to 0.574, Random Forest 0.509 to 0.582, CatBoost 0.522 to 0.564), and the tuned LightGBM's seed 42 is the highest of its five. TabNet and FT-Transformer remained below every tree ensemble after tuning (0.341 and 0.370) and were not separated from persistence. On cross-validation, the reported Random Forest configuration ranked third of 21 for its model, 0.009 below the selected one. The configuration fixed before this revision is therefore kept as the system's model, and the tuned results are reported as a sensitivity analysis.

The raw Random Forest is over-confident. Top-label ECE on the test set is 0.138 [0.082, 0.207], and the Platt slopes fitted on out-of-fold probabilities are below one for every class (0.40 for Rest, 0.22 for Normal, 0.21 for Active). Platt scaling and isotonic regression reduce ECE to 0.036 [0.012, 0.100] and 0.041 [0.019, 0.108], the multiclass Brier score from 0.509 to 0.412 and 0.410, and log loss from 0.852 to 0.697 and 0.711; the calibrated intervals exclude the raw point estimate (Figure 2, Supplementary Table S2). Calibration does not, however, improve the system. The reliability analysis on the out-of-fold days shows why. In every bin of the raw Active probability, including 0.4 to 0.7, the observed fraction of Active days lies between 3% and 17%. The raw probability therefore does not separate Active days from the rest, and any monotone calibrator maps it towards the base rate (maximum calibrated Active probability on the test set 0.10 for Platt and 0.19 for isotonic, against 0.63 raw). Under calibrated probabilities the argmax is Normal on all 192 test days (macro F1 0.281, equal to the majority baseline), and the safety layer at 0.50 never overrides anything while never issuing a Rest or Active recommendation. Calibration is therefore reported as a result and as a limitation (the forest discriminates the Active class weakly), the safety layer is kept on raw probabilities, and the deployed predictions are unchanged.

Table 8 and Figure 3 extend the device ablation to six physiological feature groups. Removing the activity and energy group (activity score, ring and watch steps, active calories) is the only change whose effect is separated from zero: macro F1 falls from 0.506 to 0.378 (paired difference 0.128 [0.016, 0.239], unadjusted for six comparisons) and F1 Active from 0.444 to 0.125, with one of eight Active days recovered instead of four. Removing the heart-rate group, which contains the leading SHAP feature, gives a difference of 0.060 [-0.015, 0.127]; the visible loss is on the Rest class (F1 Rest 0.322 to 0.158, descriptive). Removing the two composite scores from which the labels are derived leaves macro F1 unchanged (0.515; difference -0.009 [-0.089, 0.064]) and F1 Active at 0.533. The model therefore does not depend on the composite scores, while the persistence baseline, a function of the same two scores, remains at 0.304. For three groups (composite scores, sleep architecture, respiration and temperature) removal raises macro F1 by about 0.01. This is within the seed-to-seed dispersion of the full model (Section 4.11), so these differences are noise and not grounds for changing the feature set. No single group alone exceeds macro F1 0.36 (keep-one-group-in; best: heart rate 0.359); the sleep-architecture group alone is below the majority baseline, and the respiration group alone recovers no Active day. The groups are complementary: no mechanism suffices on its own, and the removal of any one but activity and energy is compensated by the others. SHAP attribution and ablation answer different questions, what the trained model uses and what cannot be compensated, and the two results (Section 4.11 and Table 8) are consistent.

Threshold sensitivity. Table 9 extends the original threshold analysis with confidence intervals, the persistence baseline and the paired difference for the settings that share the reported labeling structure. Six settings of a 3 x 3 grid are comparable (Rest threshold 55, 60, 65; Active readiness threshold 80, 85; sleep threshold fixed at 75); three of these are rows T2, T3 and T5. On all six, Random Forest stays above the persistence and majority baselines. The paired difference against persistence is separated from zero in four of six; it includes zero at Rest 55 and Rest 65 combined with Active 80, where the Active class grows to 135 days and F1 Active falls to 0.22 to 0.26 (full grid in Supplementary Figure S1). Across the six, macro F1 ranges from 0.372 to 0.506 and F1 Active from 0.222 to 0.615, and mean 24-hour heart rate is the leading SHAP feature in all six. Agreement with label-independent indicators. Table 10 compares the three classes on the 958 labeled days using watch indicators that do not enter the labeling rule and are independent of the ring scores. On the cardiorespiratory axis the labels behave as recovery states. Mean 24-hour heart rate falls monotonically from Rest (median 91.9 bpm) to Normal (86.0) to Active (81.3), with Cliff's delta for Active versus Rest of -0.84 [-0.91, -0.75], and the watch respiratory rate follows the same gradient (-0.73). Watch HRV is higher on Active than on Rest days in direction, but the interval includes zero, and Active days are not separated from Normal days on HRV; SpO2 shows no effect. On the activity axis the result contradicts the intuitive reading of the class names. Active days have fewer active calories (median 579 against 884 kcal; delta -0.42 [-0.57, -0.25]) and fewer steps (4,499 against 8,057; -0.32) than Rest days, and the day preceding an Active day also carries less activity (lag-1 deltas -0.27, -0.20, -0.20). The ring's raw signals that feed the readiness score are consistent with it, as expected (lowest nocturnal heart rate -0.88, nocturnal HRV +0.60, temperature deviation -0.27; Supplementary Table S3). The labels are therefore consistent with device-independent cardiorespiratory indicators and describe recovery rather than exertion. An Active day is a day on which the subject is recovered and ready for load, typically following a day of low activity, which is what the readiness score is designed to capture. This is the sense in which the class names, and the caloric offsets attached to them, must be read.

Table 11 and Figure 4 report the expanding-window evaluation. Performance is not stable across the four blocks and does not increase monotonically with training size. Macro F1 is 0.361, 0.377 and 0.312 on the three earlier blocks against 0.506 on the final (reported) block, and F1 Active is 0.000, 0.074 and 0.103 against 0.444. Random Forest exceeds persistence with an interval separated from zero only on the final block. On the earlier three the difference includes zero, and on the block tested on July 2024 to February 2025 the model is below persistence (0.377 against 0.412). The leading SHAP feature is mean heart rate on the first and last blocks and the readiness score on the two intermediate ones. Pooled over the 768 evaluated days, macro F1 is 0.393 [0.352, 0.435] and F1 Active 0.139 [0.052, 0.232]; this pools four models with different training histories and is not an estimate for any one of them. The reported figures thus describe the model with the longest training history evaluated on the most recent period, and the final block is the most favorable of the four. The implications are taken up in Section 5.

The SHAP analysis was replicated across data sources, feature sets and algorithms, and its stability under retraining was assessed on 100 bootstrap retrains and 100 random seeds (Section 3.8; Figure 5). Only the head of the ranking is robust. Mean 24-hour heart rate is the leading feature in 88 of 100 bootstrap retrains (in the top three in 99) and in 100 of 100 seeds; HRV balance has median rank 2 in both settings. Beyond the second rank the ordering is not stable. The top five overlap with the reported ranking by a Jaccard index of 0.49 (bootstrap) and 0.53 (seeds), typically three features in common, and Kendall's tau to the reported ranking is 0.39 [0.18, 0.59] under bootstrap and 0.75 [0.63, 0.85] under seeds. Features at ranks 3 to 7 of Figure 6 are separated by 0.00003 to 0.0005 in mean absolute SHAP, and their order is largely a property of the reported seed: active calories (rank 3) appears in the top five in 29% of seeds, deep-sleep hours (rank 7) in 86%. Mean heart rate and HRV balance are therefore presented as the robust part of the explanation, and ranks 3 to 7 as a group of features with similar importance whose order should not be interpreted. Mean 24-hour heart rate integrates daytime activity and nocturnal recovery and is a device-independent indicator of cardiovascular load; its dominance is consistent with the label validation in Section 4.7.

The confidence threshold bounds the maximum caloric error at 200 kcal (one class step) because the reported model never confuses Active with Rest. At the 0.50 threshold on the reported model, 126 of 192 test days (65.6%) receive the model's prediction and 66 fall back to Normal; the states issued are 28 Rest, 162 Normal and 2 Active. Mean caloric error against the offset of the true label falls from 71.9 kcal (no threshold) to 58.3 kcal, and macro F1 after fallback is 0.507 with accuracy 70.8%. The mean caloric error is not a standalone metric. A system that always recommends Normal has a mean error of 54.2 kcal precisely because it never issues a Rest or Active recommendation, so the error must be read together with the states issued and with F1 Active. These figures correct those in the original submission (80% coverage, 76 to 69 kcal), which had been produced with an earlier 16-feature pipeline; the correction is disclosed in the response to reviewers.
