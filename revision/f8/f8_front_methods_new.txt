Personalized nutrition depends on individual physiological data, yet most systems require continuous glucose monitors or manual food logging. This paper studies the feasibility of an explainable, knowledge-based decision support system that produces daily nutritional recommendations from passively collected data of two wearables (Oura Ring, Apple Watch). A supervised classifier assigns each day to one of three recovery states (Rest, Normal, Active) from nineteen lag-1 features, and a rule-based module with a confidence-based safety layer translates it into caloric and macronutrient targets. On a single-subject dataset of 958 days over 33 months, seven classifiers were compared on the same temporal split against majority and persistence baselines. The evaluation adds paired bootstrap intervals, calibration, SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) stability, feature-group ablation, label validation against device-independent indicators and an expanding-window temporal evaluation. The reported Random Forest reaches macro F1 0.506 [0.373, 0.610] and exceeds the persistence baseline by 0.202 [0.064, 0.317]. No difference among the tree ensembles is separated from zero, and neural tabular models are not separated from persistence after tuning. Combining the two devices raises Active-class F1 4.4-fold, and mean 24-hour heart rate is the leading SHAP feature in 88 to 100% of retrained models. Two results qualify these findings: the labels describe recovery rather than exertion, and on three earlier blocks performance (macro F1 0.31 to 0.38) is not separated from persistence. The contribution is the integrated system, its explainability and an evaluation methodology for single-subject wearable data, reported in full. The results support technical feasibility, not clinical readiness.

The system studied here is a knowledge-based decision support system for a healthcare application, built from two layers with different kinds of knowledge. A data-driven classifier supplies a daily prediction of the user's physiological state from wearable signals. A knowledge-based layer, deterministic rules grounded in nutrition guidelines together with a confidence rule that bounds the consequences of an uncertain prediction, turns that state into a personalized recommendation the user can inspect and audit. The contribution lies in this integration and in how it is evaluated, not in a new learning algorithm. The knowledge-based layer makes the recommendation explainable and its error bounded. The evaluation asks four questions: whether the data-driven component adds information beyond what the previous day's state already provides, whether its explanations are stable under retraining, whether the labels that drive the recommendation have physiological meaning, and whether the reported performance holds across time. The study is a feasibility study on a single subject, and the limits it finds are reported as results. The specific contributions are the following.

A machine learning pipeline that, to the best of our knowledge, is the first to combine Oura Ring and Apple Watch data for the classification of nutritionally relevant physiological states, with an ablation showing that the two devices are complementary (a 4.4-fold gain in F1 for the minority Active class).

An end-to-end explainable prototype that couples SHAP explanations with a rule-based recommendation module and a confidence-based safety layer, with the stable and unstable parts of the explanation identified by retraining.

An evaluation methodology for single-subject wearable data: temporal split, majority and persistence baselines, paired bootstrap comparison of seven classifiers with a reportable hyperparameter search, calibration analysis, feature-group ablation, validation of the pseudo-labels against device-independent indicators, and an expanding-window assessment of temporal robustness.

The results of that evaluation reported in full, including where the classifier does not exceed the previous day's label, where its probabilities fail to discriminate the minority class, and the finding that the labels describe recovery rather than exertion.

Each day is assigned to one of three nutritionally relevant classes using thresholds on the ring readiness and sleep scores: Rest (readiness < 60 or sleep < 60), Active (readiness >= 85 and sleep >= 75), and Normal (all other days). These thresholds are calibrated on the manufacturer's documentation, which defines scores >= 85 as optimal and scores < 70 as warranting attention. The resulting distribution is Rest 202 days (21.1%), Normal 710 days (74.1%), and Active 46 days (4.8%). We emphasize that these labels are operational pseudo-labels derived from composite scores, not externally validated clinical states; this distinction frames the interpretation of all downstream results. The class names are prescriptive, not descriptive. They denote the nutritional regime the system recommends for day t given the previous night's recovery, not the amount of physical activity measured on that day. Active denotes a high-readiness, recovered state, in which the system recommends a caloric surplus; Rest denotes a low-readiness or low-sleep state (strain), in which it recommends a deficit. Because the ring readiness score rises after days of low load and good sleep, Active days are, on average, days of lower measured activity than Rest days. This property is examined directly in Section 4.7 and is important for reading the results. The same section analyzes the sensitivity of the results to the thresholds.

In addition to Random Forest, XGBoost and the SVM, four further models were evaluated in the same pipeline during revision: LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) and CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018), both gradient-boosted trees, and two neural architectures for tabular data, TabNet (Arik & Pfister, 2021) and FT-Transformer (Gorishniy et al., 2021). All seven models receive the same temporal split, median imputation and SMOTE (k = 3) fitted on the training part; the networks and the SVM receive standardized inputs. Each model was first run with its package defaults (seed, single thread and verbosity set only) and then with a reportable hyperparameter search (Section 3.5). Because the networks have no default training length, the number of epochs was chosen without the test set: early stopping (patience 16, macro F1) on the last 154 training days, followed by a refit on the full 766-day training set for that number of epochs. All runs are deterministic on CPU (byte-identical outputs across repeated runs).

Two baselines are reported alongside the classifiers. The majority baseline predicts Normal on every day. The persistence baseline predicts, for day t, the true label of the previous day in the series (the last training day for the first test day). On this dataset it is a deterministic function of two of the nineteen features (the previous day's readiness and sleep scores), so every classifier has strictly more information than this baseline. Persistence is the natural reference for a daily physiological state that is autocorrelated, and a model that does not exceed it adds nothing beyond the previous day's label.

Hyperparameters were searched on the training period only, with a 3-fold temporal cross-validation (TimeSeriesSplit; validation blocks of 154 days at training rows 304-457, 458-611 and 612-765; imputation and SMOTE refit on each fold's training part). For Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM, LightGBM and CatBoost, 20 configurations were drawn by random search (ParameterSampler, seed 42) from spaces fixed before any model was fitted (Appendix A, Table A.1). For TabNet and FT-Transformer a fixed grid of five configurations was used, with early stopping on each fold's validation block. The configuration with the highest mean validation macro F1 was then trained on the full training set and evaluated once on the test set. The configurations reported in Sections 4.2 to 4.12 (Random Forest: 100 trees, depth 10, minimum leaf 5) were fixed before this revision. They were scored on the same folds as reference points but were not eligible for selection.

Because macro F1, accuracy, MCC and QWK do not fully characterize performance under imbalance, balanced accuracy, Cohen's kappa, the geometric mean of per-class recalls (G-mean), macro one-versus-rest ROC-AUC and macro PR-AUC are also reported. For the analyses added in revision, all confidence intervals use a single shared matrix of 1,000 bootstrap resamples of the 192 test days (seed 42), so that differences between systems can be estimated as paired bootstrap differences. The same resample is applied to both prediction vectors, and the 2.5 and 97.5 percentiles of the difference are reported; an interval that includes zero means that the difference is not separated from zero at the 95% level. Resamples in which a class is absent from the true labels are skipped and counted. Pairwise McNemar tests on per-day correctness (continuity-corrected chi-square, or exact binomial when fewer than 25 discordant days) are reported with Holm adjustment within each family of comparisons, and Cochran's Q is used for the global comparison of the three original classifiers. McNemar compares per-day accuracy and is insensitive to which class is missed, so it complements rather than replaces the paired bootstrap on macro F1. Intervals are not adjusted for multiple comparisons unless stated.

Calibration of the Random Forest probabilities was assessed because the safety layer (Section 3.13) acts on them. Out-of-fold probabilities were obtained on the training period with a 4-fold temporal cross-validation (TimeSeriesSplit; each fold's imputer, SMOTE and forest fitted only on the fold's training part), giving 612 out-of-fold days with 33 of the 38 Active training days. One-versus-rest Platt scaling and isotonic regression were fitted on these out-of-fold probabilities and applied to the test set; the forest itself was not retrained. The expected calibration error (ECE; top-label, 10 equal-width bins), the maximum calibration error, the multiclass Brier score (sum over classes, range 0 to 2) and the log loss are reported with bootstrap intervals. The per-class calibration of the Active class is given as a point estimate only (8 positives).

The stability of the SHAP ranking was assessed in two ways, separating data variability from training variability: 100 forests retrained on bootstrap samples of the 766 training days (whole pipeline refit; SMOTE and forest seed fixed), and 100 forests trained on the full training set with random seeds 0 to 99 (seed 42 being the reported model). For each retrained model the SHAP ranking was computed on the same 192 test days. Three statistics are reported: the frequency with which each feature occupies the first rank, the Jaccard overlap of the top five with the reported ranking, and Kendall's tau between rankings. SHAP attributions describe what the trained model uses; they do not establish which features are necessary. Necessity was assessed separately by a leave-one-group-out ablation (Section 3.9).

Complementing the per-device ablation (Section 4.6), the nineteen features were partitioned, before any result was seen, into six groups by physiological mechanism: composite scores (readiness, sleep score), heart rate (watch mean heart rate, ring lowest and mean nocturnal heart rate, recovery index), HRV (ring HRV, HRV balance, watch HRV), sleep architecture (deep and REM sleep hours), activity and energy (activity score, ring and watch steps, active calories), and respiration, temperature and SpO2. For each group the forest was retrained without it (leave-one-group-out) and on it alone (keep-one-group-in), with imputation and SMOTE refit on the remaining features and the reported hyperparameters. The procedure is the same as for the per-device ablation, and identity with that ablation was verified. Leave-one-group-out differences from the full model are paired bootstrap differences on the shared resampling matrix, unadjusted for the six comparisons. The composite-score group is of particular interest because the labels and the persistence baseline are functions of exactly these two scores.

The pseudo-labels were examined in two ways. First, a threshold sensitivity grid: Rest threshold in {55, 60, 65} and Active readiness threshold in {80, 85, 90}, with the Active sleep threshold fixed at 75 (nine settings, including the reported 60/85/75; the sleep threshold was not varied). For each setting the labels were regenerated on the same 958 days and the same temporal split, and the reported forest, the persistence baseline and the majority baseline were re-evaluated with the same procedure. Settings with fewer than five Active test days are reported but not interpreted. Second, agreement with indicators that do not enter the labeling rule, at two levels. Indicators from the watch (mean heart rate, HRV, active calories, steps, respiratory rate, SpO2) are independent of the ring scores that define the labels and are the only genuine validation. Raw ring signals (nocturnal HRV, lowest resting heart rate, temperature deviation, steps) do not enter the rule but feed the readiness score, and therefore show only the internal consistency of that score. For each indicator, the expected direction of the Active versus Rest difference was fixed before analysis. Medians and interquartile ranges by class on the 958 days are reported, with Kruskal-Wallis tests and epsilon-squared (Tomczak & Tomczak, 2014), Dunn post-hoc tests with Holm adjustment, and Cliff's delta for Active versus Rest with a 95% bootstrap interval (1,000 resamples within class). Consecutive days are autocorrelated, so the p-values are approximate and optimistic; inference rests on the effect sizes.

To assess whether the single temporal split is representative, the evaluation was repeated on an expanding window. Four disjoint 192-day test blocks were taken from the end of the 958-day series (the last block being the reported test set), each preceded by all earlier days as training data (766, 574, 382 and 190 days, with 38, 22, 14 and 9 Active training days). For each block the full pipeline (imputation, SMOTE, the reported forest) was refit on its training period and evaluated once. Persistence and majority baselines, bootstrap intervals and paired differences were computed as in Sections 3.5 and 3.6. The blocks are not replicates of one experiment, since training size and period differ, so no formal comparison between blocks is made; a pooled estimate over the 768 evaluated days is given for context only.

This paper presented and evaluated an explainable, knowledge-based decision support system that generates daily nutritional recommendations from passively collected smart-ring and smartwatch data. On a 33-month single-subject dataset, the reported Random Forest exceeds both a majority and a persistence baseline on the final evaluation period, the two devices are complementary (a 4.4-fold gain in Active-class F1), and mean 24-hour heart rate is the stable head of the SHAP ranking. A step double-counting artifact in a common health-data export format was characterized. The evaluation added in revision (seven classifiers with a reportable search, calibration, ablation by physiological group, label validation and an expanding-window analysis) sets the limits of these results. The tree ensembles are equivalent once tuned, and the neural tabular models are not separated from persistence after tuning. The Random Forest probabilities discriminate the Active class weakly. The labels describe recovery, not exertion. On earlier temporal blocks the model is not separated from the previous day's label. The contribution is therefore the system, its explainability and the evaluation methodology, not a transferable performance level, and the results are evidence of technical feasibility rather than clinical readiness.

Future work should prioritize multi-subject validation, which the methodology is designed to support, and nutritional validation against a reference measure of energy expenditure and against outcomes, which the present data cannot provide. Further directions are the integration of a dietary-intake module to close the feedback loop, potentially with reinforcement learning (Tripathy et al., 2024), the addition of CGM data toward metabolic personalization (Zignoli et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025), and usability studies of the explanation interface with real users (Abdelaal et al., 2024). The source code and analysis scripts will be released in a public repository with a persistent identifier.
