Principal findings. Within a single-subject design, passively collected ring and watch data support a daily three-state classification that drives differentiated nutritional recommendations. On the final evaluation period the classification exceeds both the majority and the persistence baseline; the paired difference against persistence on macro F1 is 0.202 [0.064, 0.317]. Among the seven classifiers compared, Random Forest has the highest point estimate on macro F1 at default settings. No pairwise difference among the tree ensembles is separated from zero, however, and the ordering is one of point estimates on a test set with 8 Active days. Two results are robust under retraining and across label thresholds. The first is the complementarity of the two devices, which raises Active-class F1 from 0.100 (watch only) to 0.444 (combined). The second is the dominance of mean 24-hour heart rate in the SHAP ranking, which holds in 88 to 100% of retrained models and on all comparable threshold settings, although not on all temporal blocks. The label validation adds a qualitative result that changes how the classes must be read: Active days are days of cardiovascular recovery and low measured activity, not days of high exertion. The analyses that follow establish the limits of these findings.

Temporal robustness. The figures reported in Sections 4.2 and 4.3 describe one model, trained on the full 766-day history and evaluated on the most recent 192 days. The expanding-window evaluation (Section 4.8, Table 11, Figure 4) shows that they do not describe a level of performance that holds across the observation period. On the three earlier blocks, macro F1 is 0.31 to 0.38 and F1 Active 0.00 to 0.10. The advantage over persistence is separated from zero only on the reported block and is negative on one block, and the leading SHAP feature changes on two blocks. The reported block is therefore the most favorable of the four. The reported figures should be read as the performance of the model trained on the longest history and tested on the most recent period, not as a time-invariant property of the approach. Two factors plausibly contribute, although neither was tested. The earlier training sets are small and contain few Active days (9 to 22). In addition, the subject's physiology is not stationary over the 33 months: the prevalence of Rest days varies between 15% and 31% across blocks, and 33 of the 46 Active days fall on or after July 2024. The shift of the leading SHAP feature towards the readiness score on the two intermediate blocks is consistent with a model that, given less data, leans on the composite score from which the labels are derived. The contribution of this study is consequently framed as the system, its explainability stack and the evaluation methodology, rather than as a transferable performance level. The methodology comprises temporal splits, persistence and majority baselines, label validation and paired uncertainty estimates; the qualitative findings on device complementarity and on the physiological meaning of the labels belong to the contribution in the same sense.

Choice of classifier. With seven classifiers, two baselines and a reportable search, three conclusions emerge. The three tree ensembles (Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost) perform equivalently on this dataset once tuned. Tuning offers at most about 0.06 of macro F1 over the reported configuration, with an uncertainty interval that reaches zero. The two neural tabular models, after tuning, are not separated from the persistence baseline with 766 training days, consistent with the regime described by Grinsztajn et al. (2022). The Random Forest configuration fixed before this revision is retained as the deployed model, for two reasons: the search did not identify a clearly better configuration on cross-validation, and selecting a model on the basis of test-set results would contradict the evaluation protocol. The explainability, calibration and temporal analyses in this paper all refer to that model.

Generalization from a single subject. The single-subject design is deliberate. Personalized nutrition targets the individual, and a classifier trained on one person's 33-month history answers the question whether that person's daily state can be predicted from that person's wearables; the idiographic designs reviewed in Section 2 rest on the same premise (Zignoli et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2025). The design cannot answer whether the same pipeline, thresholds and feature set would work for another person, and the results should not be read as population estimates. Three observations follow. First, the labels are calibrated to the manufacturer's score scale, which is itself personalized to the wearer's baselines, so the thresholds would need re-examination for each new subject. The participant is one physically active adult male; resting heart rate and HRV vary systematically with age and cardiovascular fitness, so the dominance of mean heart rate in the SHAP ranking and the readiness thresholds are hypotheses to re-examine on another subject, not constants of the framework. Second, the feature set and preprocessing are generic, but the expanding-window analysis shows that even within this subject performance depends on the training period and on the number of Active days available. A new subject with fewer months of data would start in the regime of the earlier blocks (macro F1 0.31 to 0.38), not of the reported one. Third, the methodology is expected to transfer. The temporal split, the two baselines, the label validation against device-independent indicators, the paired uncertainty estimates and the temporal robustness check are the elements a multi-subject study would need, and they are reusable as they stand. Multi-subject validation remains the first item of future work.

Limitations. Eight limitations bound the interpretation of these results. (1) The single-subject design and the temporal evaluation bound generalizability twice. The results reflect one individual's physiology and label baselines, and, within that individual, they hold for the final evaluation period and not for earlier ones (Section 4.8). The long observation window gives temporal volume but, as the expanding-window analysis shows, does not by itself yield a stable estimate. (2) Statistical uncertainty is substantial. The MCC interval includes zero, the ROC-AUC intervals of all three original classifiers include 0.5, no pairwise difference among the classifiers is separated from zero, and the F1 Active interval spans 0.11 to 0.70 on 8 test days. (3) The system is open-loop and has no nutritional or clinical validation. It does not observe intake, body weight or a reference measure of energy expenditure, so the recommendation layer cannot be evaluated as a prescription. The caloric offsets of +200 kcal for Active and -200 kcal for Rest are prescriptive rules, not measured effects, and the caloric error reported in Section 4.12 is measured against the offset of the true label, not against measured expenditure. Validation against a reference energy-expenditure method and against outcomes is the necessary next step and is outside the scope of the present data. (4) The classes are defined by thresholds on composite scores, an operational rather than clinical criterion. The thresholds were fixed before this revision; the reported setting has the highest macro F1 of the six comparable settings examined, with neighboring settings giving 0.46 to 0.51, and the sleep threshold was not varied. The label validation shows that the classes describe recovery rather than exertion. This is consistent with the readiness score they derive from but not with the intuitive reading of the class names; the names were retained for continuity with the system and are defined prescriptively in Section 3.2. (5) The Random Forest probabilities discriminate the Active class weakly. Calibration reduces ECE from 0.138 to 0.036 but collapses every prediction to Normal, so the safety layer operates on uncalibrated probabilities and the confidence it reports should not be read as a frequency. (6) Only the head of the SHAP ranking is stable. The order of features beyond the second rank varies across retrained models and should not be interpreted, and the leading feature itself changes on two of the four temporal blocks. (7) Two device generations were used, and a standardization-before-oversampling ordering would have been marginally preferable; both are documented but uncontrolled. (8) The caloric target relies on a single fixed coefficient (25 kcal per kilogram per day) adopted from the AI4Food protocol. It does not adapt across body-composition profiles and may correspond to maintenance or surplus rather than a deficit for some users, so the recommendation layer should be regarded as a configurable heuristic rather than a validated prescription.

Practical implications. Despite these limitations, the system demonstrates a complete recommendation pipeline driven entirely by passive data, removing the food-diary adoption barrier. It does so with a transparent decision path: a deterministic rule set, a confidence threshold with a bounded caloric error, and SHAP explanations whose stable component (cardiovascular load) can be shown to the user without over-interpreting the rest. Two findings have design consequences beyond this study. A single device is insufficient to capture the physiological state: the ring's recovery scores and the watch's continuous heart rate are complementary, and the Active class is barely detectable from either alone. A state defined from a recovery score must also be presented to the user as recovery, not as activity, because the two point in opposite directions on this subject; a recommendation interface that labels a low-activity, high-readiness day as "Active" without explanation would mislead. Finally, the evaluation methodology, in particular the persistence baseline and the expanding-window check, exposed weaknesses that a single temporal split had hidden. Both are recommended for any single-subject wearable study.
